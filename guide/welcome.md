Welcome to MSFS Blind Assist. This guide takes you from a fresh download to your first flight, and then on through every feature the app offers. It is written for blind and visually impaired pilots, and it assumes no experience with the app. If you have never flown a simulator before, that is fine too: Part 9 explains the general flying ideas the rest of the guide relies on.

## What MSFS Blind Assist Is {#gs-what-it-is}

MSFS Blind Assist (we call it BA in this guide) is a Windows application that makes Microsoft Flight Simulator usable with a screen reader and a keyboard. It runs beside the simulator, connects to it, and gives you three things a sighted pilot gets from looking at the cockpit:

- **Panels you can operate.** Every switch, knob and selector BA supports for your aircraft appears in its own window as an ordinary Windows control — a list, a button, a text box. You move through them with the arrow keys and Tab, exactly as you would in any accessible program.
- **Hotkeys you can press at any time.** Global keys read out your altitude, speed, heading, fuel and much more, and other keys open windows, set the autopilot or start guidance. They work while the simulator has the focus, so you do not need to switch windows to use them.
- **Automatic announcements and audio guidance.** BA watches the aircraft and speaks important changes — a master warning, a flight mode change, a system coming on line — and it can play steering tones that guide you along a taxiway, down a runway centreline or onto a glide path.

BA speaks through your screen reader. It works with NVDA directly, and with JAWS and other screen readers through a standard helper library. If no screen reader is running, BA falls back to Windows' built-in speech (often called SAPI), so you still hear everything.

BA works alongside your screen reader, not instead of it: when you press a button or change a list in BA's windows, your screen reader already says what happened, so BA stays quiet. [How BA Talks to You](core-concepts.md#core-speech) explains when BA does speak.

## Finding Your Way Around This Guide {#gs-using-guide}

The guide is long, because it is the complete manual. You do not need to read it from start to finish. It is built so you can jump straight to what you need using headings.

- **Parts** are the top level. Part 1 (this part) gets you set up. Part 2 opens with [a walk-through of a whole flight](core-concepts.md#concepts-story) showing how the features fit together, then explains the ideas used everywhere, such as readout hotkeys and what the tones mean. Part 3 is [the First Officer](first-officer.md), who works your aircraft's flows and checklists with you. Parts 4 to 7 follow a flight in order: planning, the ground, flying, landing, and networks such as VATSIM. Part 8 covers each aircraft. Part 9 explains general flying concepts. The appendices hold a tone cheat sheet, a glossary, troubleshooting and the full hotkey reference for every aircraft.
- On this website each chapter is its own page. The chapter title is heading level 1, **sections** are level 2 and **subsections** level 3. The [guide contents page](index.md) lists every part, chapter and section, and each chapter page ends with links to the previous and next chapters.
- The whole guide is also available as **one long page**, which is the copy that comes with BA: open the Help menu and choose User Guide, and it opens in your web browser, matching the version of BA you have installed. On that page parts are heading level 2, chapters level 3, sections level 4 and subsections level 5, and your browser's find command searches the whole guide at once.

To move by heading in your web browser:

- **NVDA:** browse mode is on by default in a web browser. Press H to go to the next heading and Shift+H for the previous one. Press 1 to 6 to jump to the next heading at that level. NVDA+F7 opens the Elements List, where you can choose Headings and see the whole outline.
- **JAWS:** press H and Shift+H to move between headings, or 1 to 6 for a particular level. Insert+F6 lists all headings so you can pick one and press Enter.

Many sections share the same pattern of sub-headings: "What it is", "When to use it", "How to use it", "What you will hear", "Settings" and "If something goes wrong". Once you know the pattern, you can jump straight to the part you want.

Some conventions used throughout:

- Words BA speaks are shown in quotes and italics, for example *"Connected to"*.
- Names of buttons, check boxes and fields are shown in bold the first time they appear, using exactly the wording on screen.
- Hotkeys are written with their mode. "Output Alt+Y" means: press the right bracket key to enter Output mode, then press Alt+Y. "Input Shift+R" means: press the left bracket key to enter Input mode, then press Shift+R. The section [Output Mode and Input Mode](getting-started.md#hotkey-modes) explains this properly, and it is worth reading carefully before anything else.
- Some features exist only on some aircraft. When that is the case, the guide says so.
